How Leak Detection Works: Finding Hidden Leaks in Sandwich, MA

A hidden leak does damage long before it shows. Detection is a process of narrowing it down, not guessing.

What leak detection actually is

Leak detection is the work of finding where water is escaping, without opening every wall to look. A good technician does not start with a tool. They start with questions about the symptoms, the age of the pipes and what has changed recently, then use equipment to confirm and narrow the location.

The reason this matters is simple. A hidden leak can run for weeks. It soaks insulation, swells framing, grows mold, and shows itself only as a high bill or a stain. Finding the source precisely means a small repair instead of a large demolition.

Signs that suggest a hidden leak

You do not need equipment to notice the first signs. They are often subtle, so it helps to know what to look for.

A rising water bill

Usage that climbs without a change in habits is the classic sign of a leak somewhere.

A meter that moves with everything off

If the dial or flow indicator keeps turning when no one is using water, water is going somewhere.

Damp, warm or stained surfaces

Wet spots on floors, a warm patch on a slab or a stain on a ceiling can mark a line below.

The sound of running water

Hissing or trickling in a quiet house points to a pressurized leak.

Step one: the meter test

The first test is free and takes a few minutes. Turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, including ice makers and irrigation. Look at your water meter. If the leak indicator or dial keeps moving, water is leaving the system.

Then shut off the main valve at the house and check the meter again. If it stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and the house, in the service line. That one result tells a plumber whether to focus on the yard or the walls.

Step two: narrowing it down

Once the area is known, technicians use equipment that finds a leak by what it does, not by what it looks like.

Why different leaks need different tools

A leak in a pressurized supply line hisses. A leak in a drain only runs when water flows. A leak in a heating line may appear only when the boiler runs. No single tool finds all of them, which is why a technician uses several and cross checks the results.

Acoustic gear works best on pressurized lines. Thermal imaging helps when hot lines are involved or when moisture creates a cooler surface. Camera inspection is the tool for drains. Matching the tool to the line type is what keeps the search short.

What a homeowner can check

  • The meter with all water off
  • Visible pipes, valves and under sink areas
  • Toilet tanks with a drop of food coloring
  • The water heater area for damp spots

What a plumber adds

  • Pressure tests that isolate zones
  • Listening gear that pinpoints a spot
  • Thermal imaging through finished surfaces
  • A repair plan that limits demolition

A quick toilet and fixture check you can do yourself

Toilet tank leaks are the most common hidden waste of water in a home. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait for a while without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. A flapper is cheap and easy to replace, and it can waste a large amount of water.

Next, look under sinks, behind the washing machine and around the water heater. Slow drips at supply connections are easy to spot and easy to fix, and they often explain a bill that seems too high.

Sandwich specifics: groundwater, wells and your meter

The water in Sandwich comes from groundwater. The Sandwich Water District draws from wells 2 through 12 and buys additional water through the Route 130 booster station from the Upper Cape Regional Water Supply Cooperative. Because it is a metered supply, a small hidden leak shows up on your bill before it shows up on your ceiling.

The Cape Cod Commission notes that the Cape's aquifer is an EPA designated sole source aquifer, and that recharge comes only from precipitation. Water here is a finite local resource, which is a reason to treat a leak as more than a billing problem.

If your meter test points to the service line, ask the Sandwich Water District which part of the line belongs to you. The district's public information does not state where ownership changes, so the question needs a direct answer from the district before any digging.

Sources: Sandwich Water District 2025 Water Quality Report via the Town of Sandwich website, and Cape Cod Commission.

Seasonal homes and leaks that go unnoticed

A leak in a house that sits empty for weeks can run unobserved the whole time. If you leave a home for the winter, shutting off the main and draining the lines removes that risk. Ask a plumber what is right for your system before you leave.

When you return, check the meter first, then walk the house looking at ceilings, floors and under sinks. A quick look on the first day catches problems that would be expensive on the tenth.

What happens after the leak is found

Once the location is confirmed, the repair is planned with the least disruption. Sometimes it is a replaced fitting. Sometimes it is a section of pipe. In older homes, repeated leaks may point to failing pipe and a conversation about replacement.

If you are in Bourne, Mashpee or anywhere nearby, our leak detection team can locate the problem, and our burst pipe repair team handles the fix when a line has failed. Dry the area quickly, because moisture left behind causes more damage than the leak itself.
