A Planned Install Beats A Failed Tank

Most water heaters give warning before they quit. Rust at the base, a rumbling burner, water that runs out faster than it used to or a tank older than a decade all point to a unit nearing the end. Installing a new one before it floods the basement gives you time to compare options and time to book a day that suits your household.

A planned installation also lets the plumber do the job in the right order. The old unit is drained, the lines are checked, the venting is inspected and the new tank is set on a proper pan with the correct drain routing. If the heater already failed and water is on the floor, see our water heater replacement page for the urgent version of this work.

Choosing Between Fuel Types

Cape Cod homes run on a mix of natural gas, propane, oil-fired boilers with indirect tanks and electricity. Natural gas in Sandwich comes through National Grid where the main reaches your street. Many homes farther out use propane or electric, so the right unit depends on what is already plumbed and what you are willing to change.

The table below compares the common options. Real operating costs shift with fuel prices, so ask us to talk through your own usage instead of relying on a general number.

Venting, Gas And Code

Venting is where most poor installs go wrong. A gas heater must exhaust safely, and a power-vent or direct-vent unit has specific pipe lengths and slopes. We follow the manufacturer's instructions and the Massachusetts plumbing code, 248 CMR 10.00, for every connection.

Plumbing and gas work in Massachusetts requires a permit pulled by a licensed plumber. In Sandwich that goes through the Building Department, which handles plumbing and gas inspections. Ask the Sandwich Building Department about the exact steps for your address. We handle the paperwork side of the job so the inspection does not become your problem.

Sandwich Water And Your New Heater

Sandwich Water District reports adding sodium hydroxide for corrosion control, and its 2025 water quality report lists lead at a 90th percentile of 0 ppb and copper at 0.399 ppm. Those results describe the district supply, not your own pipes, but they are a useful starting point when we look at how your plumbing is holding up.

The district's report does not publish hardness or pH figures, so we do not guess at them. If you have scale on fixtures or a heater that clatters, we check the unit itself and talk through whether a flush, a softener or a different model makes sense.

Details That Protect The New Unit

These small parts are easy to skip and they matter for the life of the heater.

Expansion tank

Closed plumbing systems need somewhere for heated water to expand. Without a tank, pressure spikes strain the heater and the fixtures.

Drain pan and line

A pan piped to a safe drain turns a future leak into a minor nuisance instead of a flooded floor.

Dielectric unions

Where copper meets steel, the right fittings slow down the corrosion that eats connections.

Anode rod

The sacrificial rod protects the tank lining. Checking it every few years extends the unit's life.

Unit type and size

A small electric tank, a gas power-vent unit and a heat pump hybrid sit in very different price ranges.

Venting changes

Reusing a good vent is simple. New vent runs, liners or a switch to direct vent add parts and labor.

Gas or electrical work

A new gas line, a larger line for tankless or a new electrical circuit adds to the scope.

Access and removal

Tight basements, stairs and crawl spaces change how long the removal and set take.

Code items

Expansion tanks, pans, shutoff valves and permit work are part of a compliant install.

A note on insurance

Water heater damage from a sudden failure is often a homeowner's insurance question. A planned install is usually your own cost, but check your policy if the old unit leaked and caused damage.
